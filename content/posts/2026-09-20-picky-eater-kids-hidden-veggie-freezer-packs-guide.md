# The Freezer Pack Trick for Getting Picky Kids to Eat Vegetables

Last Tuesday I watched my four-year-old pick every single pea out of her fried rice and line them up along the rim of her plate like tiny green soldiers. Twenty-three peas. She counted them. Then she ate the rice and asked for more rice. No peas.

If you've got a picky eater, you know this scene. You've tried the "hide the vegetables" recipes. You've blended cauliflower into mac and cheese at 6pm on a weeknight while a toddler screams at your ankles. And it works — sort of — except you're doing it *from scratch every single night*, which is its own special kind of exhausting.

Here's the thing nobody tells you: the cooking isn't the hard part. The hard part is doing it *daily*. The fix is to stop cooking vegetables per meal and start building a freezer stash of single-serving veggie packs on the weekend. Then dinner becomes assembly, not production.

## The weekend batch: 40 minutes, one pot, one blender

Pick 3 vegetables your kid tolerates *in any form* — usually the sweeter ones work: carrots, sweet potato, butternut squash, zucchini, peas, corn. Skip anything bitter (kale, broccoli) for now. You're building trust, not a nutrition chart.

Steam or boil them until very soft — softer than you'd eat them yourself, because texture is what most kids actually reject, not flavor. Then split the batch:

- **Puree half** in a blender with a splash of the cooking water. You want a smooth, pourable consistency, not paste. Thick puree makes lumps in sauces and kids find lumps.
- **Dice the other half** into pea-sized cubes. This is for kids past the puree stage who'll accept vegetables if they're small enough to disappear into a bite of rice or pasta.

Let everything cool completely. Warm puree in a sealer pulls moisture into the seal and you'll get a weak closure — a mistake you only make once.

## Portion it like a single meal, not a family batch

This is where most meal prep fails. People freeze one big bag of puree, then have to thaw the whole thing and use it within a couple of days. That's the same daily-cooking problem wearing a different hat.

Instead, portion into **what one meal for one kid actually needs**:

- About **2 tablespoons** of puree per serving for a toddler, **3–4 tablespoons** for a 5–8 year old.
- A small handful — roughly **1/4 cup** — of diced veggies per serving.

Spoon each portion into its own vacuum bag, flatten it into a thin, even layer (thin freezes faster and thaws in minutes), and seal. A flat, tight seal also means the bags stack like index cards instead of turning into a lumpy drawer of mystery.

Label with the vegetable and the date. They keep **3 months** at 0°F without quality loss.

## How to actually use them on a Tuesday

No defrosting the night before. No planning. Just:

1. Pull one pack from the freezer.
2. Drop the sealed bag in a bowl of warm water for **5–8 minutes**, or snip the corner and microwave the puree in a covered bowl for 30–45 seconds.
3. Stir it into something they already eat — pasta sauce, scrambled eggs, oatmeal, pancake batter, yogurt, taco meat.
4. For the diced packs, thaw and fold into rice, couscous, or a quesadilla where they blend into the filling.

The trick is **dose, not disguise**. Two tablespoons of carrot puree in a jar of marinara is invisible. Half a cup turns the sauce orange and suddenly your kid is a food critic. Start small and increase over weeks.

One more thing: keep a couple of "safe" packs — plain corn, plain peas — that you serve *as a side* without hiding them. Kids who see a vegetable regularly without pressure to eat it get comfortable with it faster than kids who only ever meet vegetables in stealth mode.

## Bottom Line

You don't need a new recipe. You need Sunday's 40 minutes to cover Tuesday's 6pm panic. Batch-cook, portion into single servings, seal them flat, and freeze. Then dinner is just reheating and stirring — and the peas never get counted again.
