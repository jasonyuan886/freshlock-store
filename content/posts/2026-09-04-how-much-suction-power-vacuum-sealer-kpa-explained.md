# How Much Suction Power Do You Actually Need? kPa Explained

Every vacuum sealer listing has a suction spec somewhere in the bullet points, usually a number followed by "kPa." Almost none of them explain what that number means or why it matters. Here's the short version: **it's the single biggest factor in how long your food actually stays fresh.**

## What kPa Actually Measures

kPa (kilopascal) measures negative pressure — how much air a pump can pull out of a sealed bag relative to normal atmospheric pressure (about 101 kPa at sea level). A vacuum sealer rated at **-60 kPa** removes roughly 95% of the air inside the bag before sealing.

The more air removed, the less oxygen is left to:
- Feed aerobic bacteria and mold growth
- Oxidize fats (the process that causes rancid flavors)
- Form ice crystals that puncture cell walls and cause freezer burn

## The Practical Ranges

| Suction Rating | What It Actually Does | Realistic Shelf-Life Gain |
|---|---|---|
| Under -30 kPa | Barely more than pressing air out by hand | +20-30% |
| -30 to -45 kPa | Removes most visible air, some residual oxygen remains | +50-100% |
| -50 to -60 kPa | Near-total air removal, minimal residual oxygen | 3-5x longer |

**-60 kPa is close to the practical ceiling for a battery-powered handheld pump.** Going lower would require a chamber sealer the size of a microwave — those hit -95 kPa+ but cost $300-2,000 and don't fit in a kitchen drawer.

## Why Listed kPa Numbers Are Often Wrong

A lot of budget sealers list a suction spec that reflects the motor's rated capacity, not what actually happens inside a bag with a standard embossed channel and a one-way valve — friction loss in the tubing and valve seat means the real number at the bag is often 15-25% lower than what's printed on the box.

Two questions cut through the marketing:
1. **Is the number measured at the bag, or at the motor?** Reputable brands specify "at the valve" or similar.
2. **Does the company publish an independent test, or just a spec sheet?** A spec sheet is marketing copy. A third-party pressure gauge test is data.

## How to Sanity-Check Suction Power Yourself

You don't need a lab. Seal a bag with nothing in it and squeeze it:
- **Rock hard, zero give:** strong suction, working as advertised
- **Some softness, air pockets you can press:** underpowered or valve isn't sealing fully
- **Bag inflates back within an hour:** valve or seal failure, not a suction problem

## Bottom Line

If you're comparing handheld vacuum sealers, treat the kPa number as the first filter, not an afterthought. Anything under -45 kPa is a marginal upgrade over a manual air-squeeze method. -60 kPa is where you get the 3-5x shelf-life improvement that actually changes how you shop and cook.
