# How to Vacuum Seal Food Without a Machine (And Why It's Worse)

You don't strictly need a vacuum sealer to remove air from a bag — there's a manual technique called the **water displacement method** that gets surprisingly close. Here's how it works and where it falls short.

## The Water Displacement Method

1. Put your food in a zip-top freezer bag (not vacuum bags — regular Ziploc-style works)
2. Seal the bag almost all the way, leaving the last inch or two open
3. Slowly lower the bag into a large bowl or sink of water, keeping the opening above the surface
4. Water pressure pushes air out through the small opening as you submerge it
5. Seal the last bit just before the opening goes underwater

Done correctly, this removes a meaningful amount of air — enough to noticeably slow freezer burn compared to just squeezing a bag shut by hand.

## The Real Limits

| Method | Air Removal | Shelf-Life Improvement | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squeeze by hand | Minimal | Roughly nothing | Low |
| Water displacement | Moderate, roughly 60-70% of air | +50-80% vs. hand-squeezed | Medium, one bag at a time |
| Handheld vacuum sealer (-60 kPa) | ~95% of air | 3-5x longer | Low, seconds per bag |

Water displacement also has practical drawbacks:
- **Messy** — wet bags, wet counters, water in the sink you're using for prep
- **Doesn't work for liquids** — soups, marinades, or juicy foods will just leak into the sink
- **Slow at scale** — fine for one bag, painful for a week's worth of meal-prep
- **No true seal** — a zip-top bag can pop open in the freezer or during transport; there's no heat-seal or valve lock

## When Water Displacement Is a Fine Choice

- You're trying vacuum sealing for the first time and don't want to buy equipment yet
- You're freezing a single item today, not doing a batch
- You genuinely don't have $75 to spend right now
